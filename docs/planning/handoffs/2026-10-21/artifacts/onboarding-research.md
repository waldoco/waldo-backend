
# Waldo onboarding: how a real assistant starts
2026-10-16 · @Suyash
A great human assistant starts with access and almost no questions: they get into the calendar and inbox, ask two or three things that cannot be seen, then learn by watching and confirm what they see with yes-or-no questions. Authority grows one area at a time, only when it is handed over. Waldo's onboarding should do the same: ask at most three things, show a real piece of work in the first session, confirm what it inferred instead of running a questionnaire, start every action at ask-first, and say plainly that it is still learning.
## The assistant model
An agent is a laptop: a capable box you configure, point at tasks and supervise. An assistant is a MacBook: it arrives set up, has opinions about how things should go, and earns more of your life by being right about the small things first. Every research track points the same way: the best human assistants ask little, watch a lot, bring finished work, and never act beyond what they were given.
|
 | Agent (laptop) | Assistant (MacBook) | | First minutes | Configure tools, set goals, write instructions | Grant access, answer two or three things, see work already done | | How it learns you | Questionnaire, settings, prompts | Watches your calendar and mail, then confirms with yes-or-no questions | | Authority | Granted up front, broad | Starts at ask-first; widens one area at a time, by your explicit yes | | How it talks | Reports what it did | Leads with the point, brings one recommendation, batches the rest | | When it errs | Logs an error | Owns it in the first sentence, has already fixed what it could, says what changes | | Personality | Neutral tool, or a costume | One steady voice: warm, brief, never servile, never cute | Two phrases from the hospitality research capture the posture: Ritz-Carlton's "Ladies and Gentlemen serving Ladies and Gentlemen" (a capable peer, not a servant), and Horst Schulze's "Elegance without warmth is arrogance."

## How real assistants start
Executive assistants, VA firms, butlers and private bankers all ramp the same way: access first, a handful of questions, weeks of watching, then authority one area at a time. Ferriss puts "real smooth sailing" at three to eight weeks; Athena markets drafting in your voice by week eight.
| Stage | What a good assistant does | What they deliberately do not do | Waldo equivalent | | Before day one | Gets calendar and inbox access ("non-negotiable", EA Campus); Ritz-Carlton prepares an arrival report from known preferences | Touch money or confidential systems | Sign in, connect Google, read the day before the first screen | | Day one | One meeting: expectations, top priorities, how you want updates, one starting process (Prialto) | Hand over everything; ask personal or health questions | Two or three questions, then a real piece of work | | Week one | Mines history: recurring meetings, who gets fast replies, what moves often; maps people; nothing delegated yet (BELAY) | Fix everything; anticipate every need | Read-only: observe, draft, propose; no side effects in the inbox | | Weeks two to four | Confirms patterns with evidence ("Overnight flights have been avoided. Still the preference?"); writes the preference doc; owns recurring tasks | Turn a routine into a rule without checking | Ask one confirmation at a time, inside the Brief or a check-in | | Days 30 to 90 | Moves from tasks to whole areas, then to decisions without checking back; weekly review asks "what are you still holding onto?" | Widen authority globally | A weekly review that offers one scoped, revocable step up | How authority grows is explicit everywhere. Michael Hyatt's five levels of delegation (do as I say, research and report, research and recommend, decide and inform, act independently) and Athena's three email levels (triage, route, chief of staff) both widen one area at a time. Ferriss turns every correction into a rule ("ADD TO RULES") and later grants "decide anything under $100 and report it". The classic failure is a mismatch: the principal meant "recommend" and the assistant acted.
## Question bank
Ask only what cannot be seen; learn the rest by watching, then confirm it with a yes-or-no question that shows the evidence. A routine is a guess until confirmed (Starkey's "pattern factor"), and anything sensitive waits for its own context.
"Interrogation is weird and gets the user to not trust and have second thoughts." (Owner, October 2026.) This is the reason for the three-question limit, and every research track agrees with it.

## How great assistants behave
They lead with the point, batch by default, bring one finished recommendation, close every loop, and keep private things private. Ten rules, each tied to what the research found:
| Rule | What Waldo does | Where it comes from | | Bottom line first, one screen | Every card opens with the answer or the ask in one sentence; detail on tap. Each item is a Decision, an Approval, an FYI or Done | Sehgal's BLUF email keywords (HBR) | | Batch, interrupt by exception | The Brief, check-ins and the Close carry almost everything. A push only if waiting makes it materially worse, and it must be answerable yes or no | Fidler on interrupting meetings; Burge's 10-minute daily huddle | | One recommendation | Two or three options at most, one marked as recommended; never an open "what do you want to do?" when he can do the work first | Doctrine of Completed Staff Work; "80% done before you bring it" | | Act only inside granted, reversible scope | Everything else is an approval card with the exact change, recipients and scope | Office Dynamics "authority explicit, not assumed"; Hyatt's levels | | Close every loop in plain words | "Done", "Waiting on Priya (asked Tuesday)", "Couldn't do it: here's why". Done only on a real receipt | EA Campus micro-behaviours | | No surprises | Raise a conflict, a missed reply or a slipped deadline the moment it is seen, before anyone else does | "Never surprise your boss" (rightattitudes); Poynter | | Unreachable means hold, not commit | Only a reversible, time-boxed hold within agreed rules, with a confirm-by time. Never send, accept or sign on inference | EA practice when the executive is travelling | | Discretion by default | Health and personal items only where you chose to see them; never in pushes, never readable by an onlooker. Urgent now, minor in the log, patterns at the weekly review | IAAP code; Fidler "assume the room hears"; nanny reporting norm | | Say no with a path | Decline against your stated priorities, never on the requester's merits, and offer an alternative | Cisco EA "I'll get you in 60 to 90 days out"; Ury | | Act quietly on what you know | Use a known preference without announcing it, but keep every remembered preference visible and editable | Ritz-Carlton guest profiles used "without calling undue attention"; ChatGPT memory controls | Voice: calm, brief, kind before clever. Schulze ranks kindness above a flawless product, because people forgive small flaws but not unkindness. Never servile, never self-congratulatory, never cute.

## When something goes wrong
Every source converges on one pattern: contain it, tell them first, own it in the first sentence, arrive with the fix already under way, then say what changes. In a study of 755 people, owning responsibility was the most important part of an apology and asking for forgiveness the least (Lewicki, Polin and Lount, 2016). Uber's field test on 1.5 million late trips found repeated apologies could be worse than none (NBER, 2019), so Waldo apologises once and makes amends with an action.
The order, every time
-
What happened, in one line.
-
Who it affects, and how.
-
What Waldo has already done.
-
The one thing needed from you, or "nothing".
-
What changes so it does not happen again.
When Waldo erred, the first word is "I". When someone else erred, say what happened and apologise for the impact, without blame (Disney's HEARD). Never grovel, explain at length, or ask for forgiveness.
How Waldo says it (drafted from the pattern, not quoted)
| Case | Waldo | | His own double booking | "I booked you twice at 3: Dana and board prep. That's my mistake. I'd keep board prep and move Dana to Thursday at 10, which she has free. Want me to send it?" | | A missed email | "I missed Priya's email on Tuesday asking for the Q3 numbers. She followed up today. I've drafted a reply with the file for you to send. From now on her emails come to the top." | | Wrong information given | "Correction: I said your flight leaves at 6:10. It's 6:40. Nothing else changes." | | Someone else's mistake | "The venue cancelled your 2pm on their side. I've found the same room five minutes away and drafted a note to the four attendees. ## What AI assistant products teach
The products that won trust showed useful work within minutes from data already connected, inferred settings and let people correct them, and never acted before people understood them. The ones that lost it over-promised autonomy, hid humans behind a persona, or filed mail away unseen.
| Product | What happened | Lesson for Waldo | | Superhuman | One-to-one onboarding calls: over 65% fully switched, about twice the activation of self-serve; full-screen required steps reached 98% completion against 30% for an optional checklist | A few required, full-screen essentials beat optional tours; consider a human call for the alpha cohort | | Fyxer | Sorts your last 300 emails right after connecting; writes your style notes itself for you to edit. Top complaints: mail "filed away without me seeing it", "emails disappear" | First value immediately, but read-only until you understand what it will do | | Howie | Praised for "inferred my defaults, then let me configure them"; a beta bug sent 147 emails to a customer's boss | Infer and confirm; approvals are a gate, never a log | | Reclaim, Sunsama | Start with one to three things, add more after a stable week; plan five to six hours of an eight-hour day at first | Start small, widen after a week | | WHOOP, Oura | Recovery stays greyed out for about four days; Oura names "the first couple of weeks" for baselines | Say "still learning", never show a weak or placeholder number | | x.ai, Clara | Human-sounding assistants with hidden human reviewers; called "unsettling" once known | Say plainly that Waldo is software; one honest voice | | Humane, Rabbit | Over-promised autonomy; more returns than sales; about 5% active | Promise less than you deliver | | Motion, Martin, Lindy | Heavy setup before any value; "overwhelming at the start"; actions reported after the fact | No configuration before the first useful output | | Gemini, ChatGPT memory | Connected apps off by default, chosen one by one; memory visible, editable and forgettable; no proactive recall of sensitive data | A visible "What Waldo knows" from day one | Apple's guidance agrees: onboarding should be "fast, fun, and optional", postpone non-essential setup, and ask for permissions in context. Microsoft's guidelines for human-AI interaction add: make clear what the system can do and how well, explain why it did something, and change cautiously over time.
## Proposed onboarding for Waldo
First run takes about two minutes and ends with real work on screen: a name, Google, a first look at today, and a Brief time. Everything else moves into the first week, asked in context, one thing at a time.
The only accent is the first look: the moment Waldo shows real work, which every research track puts inside the first session.
First run (day zero)
-
Meet. "Hello. I'm Waldo." One promise, and one honest line that Waldo is software that reads what you connect and asks before anything goes out.
-
Sign in or create. As today. The 18+ answer is recorded on the account, not just ticked.
-
What should I call you? One field, skippable.
-
Let me see your day. Connect Google, on one screen: what I read, what I always ask first (every email and every invitation), what I never do. "Continue without it" stays a real choice.
-
First look. While you watch, Waldo reads today and shows it: the next meetings, what looks tight, two or three emails that seem to need you, each with where it came from. Below it, "Here's what I noticed" (working hours, likely focus time) with a correct-me on each line. This replaces the day-shape and what-slips questions.
-
When should your Brief arrive? Quiet hours follow from the answer; volume stays Normal unless changed.
-
All set. What happens next and when ("Your first Brief is at 7:30 tomorrow"), and an honest "Give me a week to learn how you work." Ends on Home, never on a permission prompt.
First week (asked in context, at most one a day)
-
Notifications: asked right after the first Brief lands ("so the next one reaches you"), provisional by default.
-
Apple Health and the watch: offered on Body after the first Brief, with the agreements screen as today. Never in the first run.
-
Confirmations: one evidence-backed yes-or-no question inside the Brief or a check-in; each yes becomes a visible rule in "What Waldo knows".
-
Day seven: a short review of the week, ending with one scoped, revocable offer ("Want me to decline meetings that overlap your focus mornings, and tell you after?").

## Decisions and backend needs
Seven calls for the owner, then what the backend must serve for the full version.
Decisions
-
Retire "The first everyday AI agent"? The design system locks it as the category line (B13). The assistant direction needs a new line; the welcome screen already says "A personal assistant for work, life and health."
-
Let the personality north star replace the design system's tone line? B13 describes Waldo as "a calm, trusted friend who happens to have a medical degree"; the owner's direction is Alfred as a caring concierge. Proposal: the north star supersedes B13's tone and category lines; its spelling, case and banned-word rules carry over.
-
No autonomy question in the first run? B15 asks "How much should Waldo do?" with "Suggest, and let me approve" as the default. Every assistant source starts at ask-first and widens by area, by the principal's explicit yes. Proposal: state the default on the Google screen, and offer step-ups one area at a time from day seven.
-
Move Apple Health out of the first run? The old build led with health ("I read the health data your device already collects"). The research says health comes later and in context. Proposal: offer it on Body after the first Brief.
-
First look before the backend serves an agenda? Option A: wait for the agenda view. Option B: on "Show me my day", ask Waldo in Main chat for a first look now, which works today but is a chat reply rather than a designed screen.
-
A human onboarding call for the alpha cohort? Superhuman's calls doubled activation and produced five to ten bugs and requests each. Twenty minutes, logged as research.
-
How Waldo names himself. Keep "he" (B04 decision) and add one honest "I'm software" line at Meet. Hidden-human personas cost x.ai and Clara their trust.
